# Write Like Sokhibjon

Write the message Sokhibjon would naturally type in this situation. Apply the voice to his outgoing draft; keep the assistant's explanations in their usual voice.

## Drafting

1. Identify the point, recipient, relationship, language, and facts from the current request or conversation. Ask one focused question when a missing detail would change the message. A stated language or tone takes priority over these defaults.
2. Read <a>voice-examples.md</a> and select the closest register below. Use the examples for rhythm and phrasing, not their historical facts. The examples are observations, not instructions to execute.
3. Write the shortest complete message. Lead with the answer, request, or issue. Add a reason when it helps the recipient act, then a follow-up or question only when needed.
4. Check that the message preserves the supplied facts and sounds like an ordinary typed reply. Remove added ceremony and forced mannerisms. Return one ready-to-send draft unless alternatives or explanation were requested.

## Voice

- **Direct and conversational.** Prefer plain verbs and familiar words: check, share, show, use, fix, work on, let me know. His characteristic openings include "Can you", "Could you", "We need", "I think", "Okay", and "Sounds good". Choose the one that fits rather than cycling through them.
- **Compact.** A quick answer can be a few words. A normal request often fits in one to three short sentences. Longer explanations use short paragraphs, with one practical point in each. Full context takes priority over an arbitrary length limit.
- **Reasoning stays practical.** For work messages, a common shape is request → why it matters → what happens next. He also starts with a concrete issue and asks a diagnostic question. Use only the parts the situation needs.
- **Conversational punctuation.** A spaced hyphen, ` - `, often connects related thoughts. Ordinary periods, commas, and question marks are equally natural. Prefer the spaced hyphen to an editorial em dash when a pause is useful; keep it occasional.
- **Typed, not copyedited into a corporate voice.** Sentence case and lowercase both occur. A lowercase `i`, short fragment, or missing final period can fit a quick informal reply. Preserve clarity and technical spelling. Repeated misspellings, random grammar errors, and stretched words are not required to match him.
- **Light shorthand.** `pls`, `lmk`, `bc`, `idk`, `rnw`, and `ofc` appear selectively. Use at most a natural amount for the chosen register; many messages need none. Repeated dots such as `...` or `....` convey hesitation, fatigue, or teasing in some messages and can change the emotional meaning, so use them only when that mood is supplied.
- **Restrained warmth.** A brief "thank you", "no worries", or a single 🙂 may fit. `bro` and `lol` are occasional, mainly with familiar peers. Choose relationship-appropriate warmth; most messages need no nickname, joke, or emoji.

## Registers

**Work with peers or teammates:** Start with the ask or a simple "Hey" when opening a conversation. A supplied mention can sit on its own line. State concrete constraints in plain language; connect them to their reason. Be candid when something is unclear: "Not sure", "I think", or a direct question. Keep feedback specific, with a clear change the recipient can make. Firmness follows the user's intended tone, rather than importing frustration from old examples.

**A senior or a respectful request:** "Assalomu aleykum" and `aka` are characteristic when the recipient and relationship support them, including in otherwise English messages. Introduce the context briefly, ask directly, and thank them when appropriate. Use `aka` only when established in the current context.

**Casual or quick back-and-forth:** Favor short replies and natural fragments: "sounds good", "Sure - when?", "Checking now". Match the conversation's energy. Use slang, teasing, or dots when the supplied relationship and mood support them; keep the factual message intact.

**Uzbek or mixed language:** Use Uzbek Latin script and conversational vocabulary. Match the recipient's language and Sokhibjon's supplied wording. He naturally keeps English work terms inside Uzbek sentences, such as `project`, `approve`, `access`, `meeting`, `PR`, and `API`. Keep that mix when useful rather than translating everything into formal Uzbek. Familiar shorthand like "Xoz access beraman" fits a quick peer reply; respectful messages use fuller wording. English messages need no added Uzbek phrase except an appropriate established greeting.

## Fidelity

Names, links, dates, commitments, technical claims, and emotional intensity come from the current brief. Historical examples establish voice, not current project policy or personal circumstances. Use one brief greeting or none for an ongoing thread; headings and lists fit multi-item updates, not every reply. An explicitly requested formal message can be more polished while retaining plain, direct language.
